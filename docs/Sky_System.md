# 🌤️ Realtime Sky System

NeuraLink features a fully procedural, physically-inspired sky rendered entirely on the GPU using **Metal**. The sky follows the device's **real clock and place**: the sun rises and sets at the real times for where the user is, the moon stands where it really is with tonight's phase (by day too), and the stars turn through the night, the real constellations among them. The sky's colour is light scattered by a model of the real atmosphere, so sunset glows on the sun's side and twilight lingers after it.

## 1. When and where — `SkyTimeProvider` and `SkyLocation`

`SkyTimeProvider` gives the sky its instant (`now`, injectable so tests can
freeze it) and its time zone (`.autoupdatingCurrent`, so the sky follows the
user when they travel).

The place comes from the time zone, with no location permission:
`SkyLocation(timeZone:)` looks the zone's name up in `sky_zones.txt`, one row
per zone of the tz database's `zone.tab`, plus the older names iOS still
reports for some zones (`Asia/Calcutta`, `Europe/Kiev`…). `Indian/Antananarivo`
gives Antananarivo, 18.92° S, 47.52° E, wherever on the island the user is:
sunrise and sunset are right to a few minutes near the capital and only
estimates elsewhere (on the west coast, 3–4° of longitude away, about a quarter
of an hour later). Local
accuracy would need a location input, such as the planned city override in
Settings. A zone the table doesn't know (`GMT`, a
fixed offset) gets the equator, at the longitude its UTC offset stands for.

## 2. Sky Environment — `SkyColorPalette`

`SkyEnvironment.resolve(at:location:)` is the heart of the system. It works out
where the sun and moon stand (`SkyBodies`), then the colours and lights for
that (`resolve(_ bodies:)`).

### The sun, the moon and the stars — `SkyEphemeris`, `SkyBodies`

Everything comes from the instant itself, in double precision, after Meeus,
*Astronomical Algorithms*:

- **Sun** — the low-precision solar position of ch. 25, the NOAA calculator's
  (about 0.01°). Sunrise and sunset over Antananarivo land within two minutes of
  NOAA's on the solstices and equinoxes.
- **Moon** — the largest terms of the lunar theory of ch. 47 (about 0.1°),
  seen from the Earth's surface rather than its centre (up to 1° lower). Its
  lit fraction comes from the angle between it and the sun, and its light from
  that phase (a quarter moon gives about a tenth of a full moon's).
- **Stars** — the catalogue's J2000 positions, precessed to today (0.36° by
  2026) and turned by local sidereal time (ch. 12) and latitude. They rise in
  the east, the celestial pole stays put at the latitude's height, and from
  Madagascar the Southern Cross circles the south pole.

**The compass.** The scene is +Y up with the camera on +Z, looking toward
−Z past the avatar. A portrait phone sees only about 15° either side of
straight ahead, and the sun and moon spend most of their time high or off to
the sides, so the compass is turned for them: the camera looks toward the day's
sunset. The sun comes down beside the avatar every evening, in view for its last
hour, and the moons that set in the west go down there too (crescent to first
quarter in the evening, full at dawn). The sunset point is put 10° to the side
the setting sun comes down from (`SkyBodies.sunsetBearing`: right of the
avatar in the southern hemisphere, left in the northern), so she doesn't hide
it. The compass follows the sun's declination, turning under half a degree a
day, and is only ever turned about the vertical, never mirrored: the sun still
rises in the east, behind the camera. Between the tropics the noon sun can
pass a little to the other side of the zenith (in Antananarivo from late
November to mid-January).

| Antananarivo | Sunrise | Sunset | Noon sun |
|---|---|---|---|
| 21 June | 06:22 | 17:21 | 48° up, north |
| 21 December | 05:10 | 18:26 | 85° up, just south |

### Transition Factors

Two smoothstep-based factors drive every colour interpolation:

| Factor | Formula | Meaning |
|--------|---------|---------|
| `dayFactor` | `skySmooth(-0.15, 0.20, sunDir.y)` | 0 = full night → 1 = full day |
| `sunsetFactor` | `clamp(1 – abs(sunDir.y) × 3.5, 0, 1) × (sunDir.y > −0.1 ? 1 : 0)` | Peaks at sunrise/sunset, 0 mid-day & night |

The `skySmooth` helper is a classic **smoothstep** (`t² (3−2t)`) giving a perceptually natural S-curve transition instead of a harsh linear ramp.

### The atmosphere — `SkyAtmosphere`

The sky's colour is sunlight (and moonlight) scattered by the air, after
Hillaire's model ("A Scalable and Production Ready Sky and Atmosphere Rendering
Technique", 2020), the one Unreal's sky uses. The air scatters blue most
(Rayleigh), fine aerosols scatter all colours forward around the sun (Mie, the
glow), and the ozone layer takes out some red and green, which keeps the
twilight zenith blue. Each thins with height. The GPU keeps three lookup tables
(`SkyAtmosphere.metal`):

| Table | Size | Holds | Built |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transmittance | 256×64 | How much light gets from any height, along any direction, to space | Once |
| Multiple scattering | 32×32 | Light scattered more than once, which fills the shadowed side of the sky and keeps twilight from going black | Once |
| Sky view | 192×108 | The whole sky seen from the ground, lit by the sun and the moon; in alpha, the haze in front of the clouds | When either moves more than 0.1° (about every 25 s) |

The sky's fragment shader then only looks the view up. What follows from it,
none of it hand-keyed: the sky whitens toward the horizon and brightens around
the sun; at sunset the glow sits on the sun's side, and the opposite sky turns
pink over the blue band of the Earth's own shadow; after sunset an orange band
stays where the sun went down while the sky above deepens through purple to
blue.

**The eye's adaptation.** Twilight is a hundred to ten thousand times darker
than day, so the sky is brightened as it darkens, as an eye adapts:
`SkyAtmosphere.exposure` follows the sky the eye looks at, the glow low toward
the sun plus the sky overhead. That holds steady from mid-morning to noon and
dips a little at sunrise and sunset, when the glow by the low sun is brightest.
Through twilight it makes up for 80% of the darkening, so the sky still
deepens: six degrees after sunset the sky is still lit, about a third as bright
as by day. It stops adapting at 0.03% of the day's sky, around 10° down, where
the last of the twilight fades into the night.

**The night.** A physical sky goes black after twilight. Under it, the night's
own glow (airglow and starlight) keeps the night palette, deep blue and a
little brighter overhead (`nightGlowLow` `#0d1321`, `nightGlowHigh` `#1c2331`),
coming in from the sun 1° down to 9°. The moon lights the air like a weak sun,
so a full moon brings a faint blue sky around and above it
(`SkyRenderer.moonSkyLight`, scaled by its brightness).

**The grade.** The table holds light, not colours: the shader shows it through
a soft shoulder (`1 − e^−x`), so the bright sky by the sun rolls off instead of
clipping, and lifts its saturation a little, toward the toon-shaded avatar in
front of it.

**The sun's colour.** `SkyAtmosphere.lightColor` is the sunlight left after
the air it crosses, its brightest channel 1: white-gold overhead, orange low,
red on the horizon. The sun's disc takes it at the sun's height (drawn over the
glow, less deep, so a red sun still shows against it), and the key light at its
own, never lower than its 7° floor.

### Three-Point Lighting for VRM Models

The resolved `SkyEnvironment` also drives the three-point light rig that illuminates the VRM character so the model always looks natural against the sky:

| Light | Source | Day colour | Night colour |
|-------|--------|-----------|-------------|
| **Key** | `SkyKeyLight`: sun by day, moon (or the sky above) by night, on the camera's side | The sunlight through the air: white-gold high, orange low | Cool blue `#8c99d9` |
| **Fill** | Opposite side of key, angled from sky | The sky's colour on that side (`SkyAmbient`), as bright as the old sky blue | The same, as dim as the old night blue |
| **Rim** | Above-behind the model; the key light itself while that stands behind her | The sky behind her, as the camera sees her against it; the key's colour while that stands behind her | The same |
| **Ambient** | The whole sky and the ground under it (`SkyAmbient`), MToon's GI | Blue from above, the sand's warmth from below | Cool, from the night's glow and the moonlit sky |

> **Why a moon key light?** At night `dayFactor` = 0, so the key light smoothly transitions to a cool-blue night light: from the moon while it is up and more than a crescent, from high in the sky otherwise. Its intensity follows the moon's brightness, 0.42 on a moonless night to 0.58 under a high full moon, so the character stays readable without breaking the night-sky illusion.

**One key light, no flip.** `SkyEnvironment.keyLight` is the only light the
terrain, its shadows (the avatar's among them) and the 3D environments follow.
Through twilight it travels from the sun to the night light *over the sky*
rather than jumping to the opposite horizon when the sun crosses it, so
shadows shorten and turn instead of swinging across in one frame. It never
drops below about 7° of elevation (`SkyKeyLight.minimumElevation`), where a
grazing light would throw endless shadows. Its largest turn between two
consecutive minutes is under 8°.

**The avatar's face stays lit.** Facing the sunset puts the afternoon and
evening sun behind the avatar. She takes the key light mirrored onto the camera's
side (`SkyKeyLight.avatarDirection`), so her face is lit warm while the sun goes
down beside her, and her self-shadow follows that light. The real light rims her
instead: while it stands behind her, the rim light takes its direction and
colour. It stays modest because MToon shares its lights out, and a strong back
light would take the face's. Her shadow on the ground still falls from the real
key light (`keyLight.direction`, not the mirrored one): from the sun by day,
from the night light or the twilight blend between them otherwise.

### The sky's own light — `SkyAmbient`

MToon's GI is the spec's SH probe: `gi = lerp(SH(normal), SH average,
giEqualizationFactor)`. Here that probe is the sky itself, at the lowest order
that has a direction (L1 spherical harmonics, convolved with the cosine). Each
time the sky view is lit again, `sky_ambient_sh` (one threadgroup of 256) sums
the sky *as shown* (graded, the night's glow in) into a mean and a gradient
along each axis:

```
mean     = 1/4π ∫ L dω
gradient = 1/2π ∫ L ω dω          GI(n) = mean + gradient · n
```

The GPU sums the upper hemisphere. `SkyRenderer` reads the result back a frame
or so later, once its command buffer has finished, and adds the ground: a
plane of the terrain's sand (`groundAlbedo`) lit by that sky and by the sun.
For a uniform lower hemisphere that adds half its light to the mean and takes
half off the gradient along +Y.

The avatar takes the sky's colour and the direction it comes from, but not its
brightness. `SkyAmbient.shaped` scales it to the luminance of the old ambient
curve, which keeps a toon-shaded face readable at night. It keeps 80% of the
colour and limits the gradient so no side falls below 40% of the mean. The
result is blue from above at noon, orange from the sun's side at dusk, and
cool at night, with the face as bright as before. Rain drains and darkens it
like the sky (`overcast`). Without a sky yet, it falls back to the old
hemisphere: one colour, 35% brighter from above and 35% darker from below. The
`Uniforms` carry the mean in `ambientColor` and the gradient in three
`ambientAlong` fields after the old layout (480 bytes), for both the main and
outline passes.

The fill and the rim take their colour from the same light: the sky on the
fill's side, and the sky behind the avatar. The fill stays as strong as it
was. MToon averages its lights by strength, so without it the key's share
grows and the lit half of a face at noon blows out.

**The ground and the environments read it too.** The terrain colours its
sunlit part with the key light (white-gold by day, orange at sunset, cool under
the moon). Where the sun doesn't reach, it takes a quarter of the sky's hue
from above; more would drain the sand's warmth. Outdoor 3D environments keep
their palette's brightness but take the sky's hue along each surface's normal.
Interiors (`EnvironmentOption.isInterior`) keep their own palette.

### Wind — `SkyWind`

A light breeze moves the hair and clothes. It blows the way the clouds drift,
wandering about ±35° over minutes, in slow, uneven gusts that never quite
stop. It is stronger on a windy afternoon (the clouds' speed) and in the rain,
up to 1.8×. It enters `SpringBoneSolver` as one term beside gravity:

```
nextTail += wind · dt · dragForce
```

That is the spec's own drag, slowing a tail relative to the moving air rather
than to still air. Stiff chains barely move and light hair sways. With the wind
at zero, the step is exactly the spec's. It is NeuraLink's own extension: on by
default for the bundled characters, whose springs it was tuned on, and opt-in
for imported ones. Both switches are in User Profile → Environment Settings
(*Wind in Hair and Clothes*, *Wind for Imported Characters*).

## 3. GPU Uniforms — `SkyUniforms`

`SkyUniformsData` is a **256-byte flat struct** shared between Swift and Metal. The layout is fixed and must match `SkyUniforms` in `SkyShader.metal` exactly:

| Field | Bytes | Offset | Description |
|-------|-------|--------|-------------|
| `invViewProjection` | 64 | 0 | Camera inverse VP matrix — maps NDC → world ray |
| `sunDirectionAndIntensity` | 16 | 64 | `xyz` = toward-sun unit vector, `w` = key intensity |
| `sunColorAndSize` | 16 | 80 | `xyz` = disc colour, `w` = disc exponent (300 = tight) |
| `nightGlowLow` | 16 | 96 | `xyz` = the night's glow at the horizon (weighted by how dark it is) · `w` = rain |
| `nightGlowHigh` | 16 | 112 | `xyz` = the night's glow at the zenith |
| `cloudParams` | 16 | 128 | `x` = elapsed time · `y` = scroll speed · `z` = coverage · `w` = star visibility |
| `moonDirectionAndPhase` | 16 | 144 | `xyz` = toward-moon unit vector, `w` = lit fraction |
| `bodyParams` | 16 | 160 | `x` = moon angular radius (radians) · `z` = moon brightness against full |
| `starRotation` | 48 | 176 | `float3x3`: star catalogue (J2000 equatorial) → world; its transpose takes a view ray back into the stars' frame |
| `moonEdges` | 16 | 224 | cos of the moon's angular radius × 1.48 (where it starts hiding stars), × 1 (where it hides them all), × 1.03 and × 0.97 (its disc's soft edge): the same for every pixel, so worked out once on the CPU |
| `detail` | 16 | 240 | `x` = cloud layers drawn: 2, or 1 on a hot phone (see [Cost](#cost)) |

The buffer is allocated once with `.storageModeShared` (CPU-visible Metal memory) and updated every frame via `copyMemory`.

## 4. Sky Renderer — `SkyRenderer`

`SkyRenderer` owns the Metal pipelines and the atmosphere's tables, and runs in
three steps:

### Per-Frame Update (Main Thread)
```swift
func update(deltaTime: Float)
```
- Advances `cloudTime` (the cloud animation clock).
- Calls `SkyEnvironment.resolve(at: timeProvider.now(), location: timeProvider.location)` to get a fresh environment.

### The Atmosphere's Tables (Before the Render Pass)
```swift
func encodeAtmosphere(commandBuffer: MTLCommandBuffer)
```
Builds the transmittance and multiple-scattering tables the first time, with
the clouds' noise (256², `r16Float`, then its mipmaps by a blit), and lights the
sky view again once the sun or the moon has moved 0.1° (or the moon's
brightness changed), with the sun's light at the eye's exposure for its height.
The sky view's alpha holds the haze in front of the clouds (see ④). Each new
sky view is also summed into the sky's light (`sky_ambient_sh`), which comes
back as `skyAmbient` on a later frame (`collectSkyAmbient`) and goes into the
next `SkyEnvironment`.
`VRMRenderer` encodes it with the shadow passes, before the main render pass, on
every frame path. Until the sky view has been lit once, the sky isn't drawn.

### Render Thread Preparation
```swift
func prepareForEncoding(viewProjection: simd_float4x4)
```
Packs the latest `SkyEnvironment` into `SkyUniformsData` and `copyMemory`s it into the shared Metal buffer. This must be called from the render thread, immediately before `encode`.

### Draw Call
```swift
func encode(encoder: MTLRenderCommandEncoder)
```
Issues a **single 3-vertex draw** — a fullscreen triangle, with the sky-view
table, the transmittance table and the clouds' noise as its textures, and the
sky view's last lights (`SkyViewParams`) and the atmosphere's constants as
bytes, for the clouds. No vertex buffer is needed; vertex positions are hardcoded in the vertex shader using the `vertex_id` built-in. The vertex shader also works out the world-space view ray at each corner, through the inverse view-projection matrix. That ray is affine in screen space for a perspective or orthographic camera, so interpolating it is exact, and the fragment only normalises it. The depth state is set to **always pass / no write**, ensuring the sky only fills pixels behind all other geometry.

While stars are out, a second draw adds the bright-star catalogue on top: one
instanced 4-vertex strip per star (about 300), blended additively, reading the
clouds' noise to hide the stars behind them. It is skipped
by day, and under an orthographic camera, which has no sky to place them in.

> The sky must be encoded **before** VRM draw calls so it renders behind the model without a depth conflict.

## 5. Metal Shader — `SkyShader.metal`

The sky is two draws. The fullscreen fragment shader (`sky_fragment`)
takes the **world-space view ray** interpolated from the vertices, then
composites layers 1–6 from back to front. The
catalogue stars (7) are a second, instanced draw on top, through their own
`sky_star_vertex` / `sky_star_fragment`:

### Layer Stack (back → front)

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  1. Atmosphere — sky-view   (always)        │
│  2. The Night's Glow        (after sunset)  │
│  3. Faint Stars             (night only)    │
│  4. Clouds — two lit layers                 │
│  5. Sun Disc + Bloom        (above horizon) │
│  6. Moon — phase-lit sphere (when it's up)  │
│  7. Bright Stars — sprites  (second draw)   │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

#### ① Atmosphere
```metal
float4 behind = skyView.sample(around, skyViewUV(viewDir));   // a = the haze before the clouds
float3 colour = skyAtmosphere(behind.rgb, viewDir, sky);      // the grade + the night's glow
```
The view ray's place in the sky-view table: around the horizon by azimuth, and
up from straight down to straight up, squeezed toward the horizon, where the
sky changes fastest. Shown through the grade (a soft shoulder, a little more
saturation). Below the horizon the table holds the haze of the air in front of
the ground.

#### ② The Night's Glow
The night palette, horizon to zenith, weighted by how far the sun is down,
added under the twilight, and darkened to 22% below the horizon. Rain then
drains the colour and darkens the sky and its clouds (`skyOvercast`, after ④),
keeping its brightness otherwise, so a rainy night stays night.

#### ③ Faint Stars
The faint stars are procedural, but in the stars' own frame: the view ray is
turned back by `transpose(starRotation)`, so they turn with the sky. A grid on
the faces of a cube holds at most one star a cell, kept inside it; the chance
of one follows the cell's solid angle, so the cube's corners are no denser than
its faces, and each star is a round dot of a set angular size (0.09–0.16°).
Visibility is gated on `starVis`, which reaches 1 only when the sun is well
below the horizon, and the moon's glare washes them out (more near it, and more
the fuller it is).

#### ④ Clouds (two lit layers)
Two layers, each a shell around the planet, so they run on to a real horizon,
smaller with distance, instead of being stretched over a dome:

| Layer | Height | Noise cell | At the day's coverage | Opacity |
|-------|--------|------------|-----------------------|---------|
| Low   | 2 km   | 4.5 km     | Heaped clouds over about two fifths of the sky; a full deck in the rain | 100 % |
| High  | 6 km   | 26 km      | A thin veil | 75 % |

**Shape.** `sky_cloud_noise` bakes a tiling noise once at launch: value noise
in five octaves, each wrapping at the texture's edges, with mipmaps. Each layer
reads it twice per pixel, the second time 3.7× finer and turned 37° to fray the
edges. Two layers of six octaves of hash noise used to run here, about 50 hash
evaluations a pixel. The mip level follows the pixel's footprint, so far clouds
don't shimmer. Coverage (`cloudParams.z`) sets the threshold the noise has to
pass: 0.50 by day, 0.60 at sunset (`0.50 + sunsetFactor × 0.10`), and 0.92 in
the rain. Both layers drift with `cloudTime × cloudSpeed`, and the speed peaks
at noon (`0.008 + dayFactor × 0.018`).

**Light.** The sun lights the clouds until it has set for them too, after it
has set for the ground: from 2 km up it stays in view to 1.4° below the
horizon, and from 6 km to 2.4°. After that the moon lights them. The light
reaching a cloud comes from the transmittance table at the cloud's own height,
so it is white overhead, orange low and red at the end. The sky view's
exposure is folded in, so a cloud stands out from the sky as much as it would
to the eye. How much of that light reaches the cloud's underside depends on
its own thickness and two more reads toward the light, spaced farther apart
the lower the light stands. A low sun lights the side of a cloud that faces it and leaves the rest
grey, and a thick core, or a rain deck, is darker than the edges. A
Henyey–Greenstein forward peak (g = 0.6) lines the thin edges toward the light
with silver.

The shadowed side takes the sky's own light (behind the cloud and overhead)
and a little from the ground below. At night the clouds take slightly more of
the night's glow than the sky does, so they read grey-blue against it, and
silver near a full moon.

**Haze.** The sky view's alpha holds how much of the low layer shows through
the air in front of it: the transmittance from the eye to the layer, from the
transmittance table. It is about 1 overhead, under a half on the horizon
(150 km away), and 0 below the ground. A cloud is mixed over the sky by its
density × that, so far clouds fade into the haze in the haze's colour. The
high layer takes the alpha to the power 1.3.

**What's behind them.** The layers composite back to front. Their cover
(`clouds`) dims the sun, the moon and their glows. The catalogue stars take
the same cover, read from the coarse noise at a fixed mip level.

#### ⑤ Sun Disc + Bloom
```metal
float cosG  = max(dot(viewDir, sunDir), 0.0);           // nothing away from the sun
float disc  = pow(cosG, discExponent);                   // tight disc
float bloom = pow(cosG, 18.0) * 0.15;                    // a touch of corona
colour = mix(colour, sqrt(sunColor), saturate(disc * 1.5) * discClear);
colour += sunColor * bloom * glowClear;
```
`discExponent` is hardcoded to **300** giving a stylised but bright disc. It is laid *over* the sky rather than added, so an orange or red sun still shows against the bright glow around it, in a colour less deep than the light it gives (`sqrt`), as the eye sees a sun it can't look at. The atmosphere's own forward scattering makes the wide glow; the bloom only adds a touch. The sun sits behind the clouds: `discClear = 1 − 0.95 × clouds` hides the disc under a thick cloud, and the glow carries through more (`glowClear = 1 − 0.6 × clouds`). The disc takes the sun's own colour (`SkyEnvironment.sunColor`), not the key light's.

#### ⑥ Moon
The moon is drawn at its own position (`moonDirectionAndPhase`), at a stylised
size of about five times the real one (`SkyRenderer.moonAngularRadius`). Each
pixel of the disc is a point on a sphere lit from the sun's direction, so the
phase, and the tilt of its horns, follow from where the two stand: a first
quarter moon is lit on its sunward side in the afternoon and from below as it
sets. Darker seas are fixed to its face. At night the unlit part takes a faint
earthshine (strongest under a thin crescent) and still hides the stars behind
it, and a glow scaled by the moon's brightness surrounds it. By day it shows
whenever it is up, part-transparent, so its seas take the blue of the sky. The
clouds in front dim it, like the sun.

#### ⑦ Bright Stars
The ~300 catalogue stars are sprites (`sky_star_vertex` / `sky_star_fragment`):
small camera-facing quads at the star's turned direction, sized and brightened
by magnitude (Sirius largest) and tinted by colour index, from blue-white to
orange. Everything that dims one is worked out once per star in the vertex
shader: daylight, the horizon (dimmer and twinkling through the low air), the
moon in front of it or its glare, and the clouds.

## 🌧️ Procedural Rain System

The rain system in NeuraLink is a two-tier GPU-driven effect that simulates atmospheric depth and camera-lens proximity.

### 1. 3D World Rain (Streaks)
- **Shader**: `RainShader.metal` (vertex-driven)
- **Technique**: Millions of tiny streaks are procedurally positioned in a 3D volume around the camera.
- **Physics**: Falling speed and slant are controlled via `RainUniforms` to simulate wind.
- **Efficiency**: No geometry is stored; the vertex shader uses the `vertex_id` to generate positions on-the-fly using a high-speed hash function.

### 2. 2D Lens Splashing (Ripples)
- **Shader**: `RainOverlay.metal` (fragment-driven)
- **Technique**: A full-screen overlay that simulates raindrops hitting the "camera lens".
- **Visuals**: Uses procedural noise to generate ripple distortions and refractive "beads" of water that drip down the screen.

### 3. Weather Synchronization
The `RainController` manages the global `intensity` (0.0 to 1.0).
- **Opacity**: Both the 3D streaks and 2D splashes scale their alpha based on intensity.
- **MToon Sync**: The character's materials automatically darken and gain a specular sheen as rain intensity increases, simulating wet fabric and skin.

## Cost

The sky is one fullscreen pass and the catalogue-star sprites, drawn before
everything else. On a phone's tiled GPU, the pixels the terrain and the avatar
cover later are never shaded. Per pixel, the pass does only what shows:

- The view ray comes from the vertices.
- With no clouds there is no cloud work: coverage is the same for every pixel.
- The sun's terms run only within 39° of it, and the moon's within 37°;
  further out they are under a 255th.
- The moon's edges are cosines worked out once on the CPU, and the faint stars
  use an integer hash rather than a sine.
- The atmosphere's tables are rebuilt when a light moves 0.1° (about every
  25 s), not every frame, and the clouds' noise is baked once.

Measured over the whole screen at the iPhone 11's resolution (828×1792),
natively on an Intel UHD 630, a GPU of about the iPhone 11's class: 1.3 ms
cloudless, 2.4 ms on a cloudy day, 2.7 ms at night. On the phone, the half of
the screen behind the terrain and the avatar is skipped. Read the device's own
figure from the `SkyBackground` group in a Metal System Trace.

**On a hot phone.** At `RuntimePressurePolicy`'s reduced and minimal tiers
(serious heat or Low Power Mode, and critical heat), the sky view is lit again
every 0.2° instead of every 0.1°, so half as often. Only the low cloud layer is
drawn; the high veil goes. A cloudy day then measures 1.9 ms.

## Checking the sky

- **Run the clock elsewhere.** Set `NL_SKY_DATE` (`yyyy-MM-dd`) and/or
  `NL_SKY_TIME` (`HH:mm`) in the scheme's environment to start the sky's clock
  at that moment, and `NL_SKY_SPEED` to run it faster (`60` = a minute a
  second). `NL_SKY_ZONE` (an IANA name such as `Europe/Paris`) moves the sky to
  that zone's city and reads the date and time there. Use them to watch a dawn,
  a moonrise or the other hemisphere on a device without waiting.
- **Snapshots.** `SkySnapshots` (env-gated by `NL_SKY_SNAPSHOT_DIR`) renders Oz
  on the open terrain at fixed dates and times in Antananarivo: a first-quarter
  evening, a full moon and a waxing gibbous. It takes the figure, the sky toward
  the light and the sky away from it, the moon close up whenever it is up, and at
  night the sky toward the celestial pole (the Southern Cross and the Pointers).
  It also writes the sun, moon and key-light angles and the moon's lit fraction,
  plus the GPU time of the sky pass alone at a cloudy noon and a moonlit night,
  to `report.txt`, and the first day's noon and dusk again in the rain
  (`*_rain.png`). Through the simulator that time is the Mac's, and not to be
  trusted; on a device, the pass shows up as `SkyBackground` in a Metal System
  Trace.
- **The gallery.** With `NL_SKY_DOC_DIR` set to the absolute path of
  `docs/Sky` instead, `SkySnapshots` rewrites the pictures at the top of this
  page: a winter's day toward the sun and Oz's face through it, the moon
  through the lunation that starts at the June new moon, and the Southern
  Cross on that moonless night. Regenerate them whenever the sky's look changes.
